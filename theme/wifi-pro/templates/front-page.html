A short history

Where Wi-Fi came from

1985

The spectrum opens

The US regulator (FCC) allowed unlicensed use of the 900 MHz, 2.4 GHz and 5.8 GHz bands for spread-spectrum radios. Without this decision there would be no Wi-Fi.

Late 1980s

The first wireless LANs

NCR, later part of AT&T, developed WaveLAN in the Netherlands. Vic Hayes from that team went on to chair the IEEE 802.11 working group for a decade and is often called the father of Wi-Fi.

Early 1990s

Solving the echo problem

In Australia, a CSIRO team led by John O'Sullivan tackled the biggest obstacle indoors: radio signals bouncing off walls and arriving as smeared echoes. Their patented approach, building on OFDM and error correction, became important to later Wi-Fi standards.

1997

The first standard

IEEE 802.11 was published, offering 1 and 2 Mbps in the 2.4 GHz band.

1999

Wi-Fi gets a name

802.11b raised speeds to 11 Mbps at 2.4 GHz, and 802.11a introduced OFDM at 54 Mbps in 5 GHz. The industry group that became the Wi-Fi Alliance formed the same year, and the name "Wi-Fi" was born.

2003 to 2013

Faster and smarter

802.11g brought 54 Mbps to 2.4 GHz. 802.11n (now called Wi-Fi 4) added MIMO, sending several data streams at once. 802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5) moved to wider 5 GHz channels and added multi-user MIMO.

2019 onward

Efficiency and new spectrum

Wi-Fi 6 introduced OFDMA to serve many devices at once, and Wi-Fi 6E opened the 6 GHz band. Wi-Fi 7, certified from 2024, adds 320 MHz channels, 4096-QAM and multi-link operation across bands at the same time.

Each generation solved a real problem: interference, echoes, crowded spectrum, too many devices. Understanding those problems is still the best way to troubleshoot Wi-Fi today.
